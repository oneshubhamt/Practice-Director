Passage for questions 5 to 8

For most of the twentieth century, forest managers treated fire as an enemy to be defeated. Every blaze was to be out by ten o'clock the next morning, and for decades the policy appeared to work: fewer hectares burned, and the forests grew denser.

The appearance was misleading. In many dry forests, frequent low fires had once cleared the undergrowth every few years, leaving the large trees unharmed and wide spaces between them. With fire excluded, small trees and dead wood accumulated. When fires did escape control, as some always do, they no longer crept along the ground. They climbed this ladder of fuel into the crowns and killed trees that had survived centuries of earlier burning.

The lesson usually drawn is that suppression failed. A more careful reading is that it succeeded too well at the wrong task. It reduced the number of fires while increasing the severity of the few that mattered, and it was judged by the first measure alone.
